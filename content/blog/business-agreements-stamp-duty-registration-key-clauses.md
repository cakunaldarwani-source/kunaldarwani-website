A business agreement that is not properly stamped, or not registered when registration is required, may not be usable as evidence when you need it most. And an agreement that ignores tax can cost more than the deal is worth. Here is what to watch.

## Stamp duty: why it matters

Under the Indian Stamp Act, 1899 as it applies in each state, an instrument that is **not duly stamped** cannot be admitted in evidence until the deficit duty and a penalty are paid, which can be up to ten times the deficit. Stamp duty must be paid **before or at the time of execution**.

- In Uttar Pradesh, stamp duty is paid through **e-stamping** (Stock Holding Corporation of India) or through the state's online portal.
- Duty depends on the **type of instrument**: partnership deed, lease, gift, agreement, power of attorney and so on. For many instruments it also depends on the value.
- Leases are charged on the average annual rent, the premium and the deposit, depending on the term.
- Gift deeds of immovable property attract duty on the market value. Concessions exist for gifts to certain family members in some states, including Uttar Pradesh.

## Registration

Under Section 17 of the Registration Act, 1908, registration with the Sub-Registrar is **compulsory** for:

- leases of immovable property **for more than one year**, or reserving a yearly rent
- gifts of immovable property
- instruments that create, transfer or extinguish rights in immovable property worth ₹100 or more

An unregistered document that requires registration does not affect the property and generally cannot be received as evidence of the transaction. This is why many rent agreements are written for 11 months. Even so, a document for a shorter term must still be correctly stamped.

## Clauses that most often cause disputes

1. **Scope and deliverables** – vague descriptions lead to arguments about what was promised.
2. **Payment terms** – amounts, due dates, taxes (whether GST is extra), and interest on delay.
3. **Term, renewal and termination** – the notice period, termination for cause, and what survives termination.
4. **Exit in partnerships and JVs** – how a partner or shareholder exits, how their share is valued, and transfer restrictions such as right of first refusal, tag-along and drag-along.
5. **Intellectual property** – who owns work created under the agreement.
6. **Confidentiality and non-solicitation.**
7. **Dispute resolution** – the jurisdiction, and whether disputes go to arbitration, with the seat and number of arbitrators.

## Tax angles in common agreements

| Agreement | Tax points |
|---|---|
| **Partnership deed / LLP agreement** | Remuneration and interest to partners must be authorised and quantified in the deed to be deductible. TDS applies on partner payments above ₹20,000 a year. |
| **Lease / rent** | TDS on rent above the threshold; GST on commercial rent (and under reverse charge in some cases where the landlord is unregistered). |
| **Franchise** | GST on franchise fees and royalty; TDS on royalty; transfer pricing if the franchisor is a related party. |
| **Consultancy / professional services** | TDS on professional fees; GST registration status of the consultant. |
| **Employment** | Treatment of perquisites, notice pay and non-compete payments. |
| **Gift / family arrangement** | Exemption for gifts from relatives; clubbing of income; stamp duty concessions. |

## An execution checklist

1. Agree the final draft, and check that the names of the parties match their PAN, GST and incorporation records.
2. Work out the duty for the type of instrument and its value, and buy the e-stamp certificate in the name of one of the parties before signing.
3. Print the agreement on, or attach it to, the e-stamp certificate.
4. Have every page initialled and the last page signed by authorised signatories, with two witnesses where the law requires it.
5. Attach board or partner resolutions authorising the signatory.
6. Where registration is compulsory, present the document before the Sub-Registrar within the time allowed under the Registration Act.
7. Keep the original safely and scan a full copy, including the stamp certificate.

## What we commonly see in practice

Most problems surface only when a dispute or a tax scrutiny begins. A partnership deed is changed by an unstamped supplementary letter, so the revised remuneration clause cannot be relied on. A lease runs on long after the original eleven months with no fresh document, and the security deposit and rent increase are recorded only in emails. Agreements signed by an employee who had no written authority are questioned by the other side. And GST is left out of the payment clause, leading to an argument about whether the price was inclusive.
