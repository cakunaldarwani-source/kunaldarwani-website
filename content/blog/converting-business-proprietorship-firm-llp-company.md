Conversion lets a business change its legal form while keeping its assets, liabilities, contracts and history. Without care, though, a conversion can be treated as a **transfer** for income tax purposes, which triggers capital gains, and it can create stamp duty and GST costs. Here is a guide to the common routes.

## The common conversion routes

| From → To | Legal route |
|---|---|
| Proprietorship → Private company | Business transfer to a newly incorporated company (succession) |
| Partnership firm → LLP | Section 55 and Schedule II of the LLP Act |
| Partnership firm → Company | Part I of Chapter XXI (Section 366) of the Companies Act |
| Private company → LLP | Section 56 and Schedule III of the LLP Act |
| OPC → Private / public company | Rule 6 of the Incorporation Rules (no conditions since 2021) |
| Private ↔ Public company | Section 14 / Section 18 – alteration of articles and fresh certificate |

## Tax-neutrality conditions

The Income-tax Act exempts certain conversions from capital gains, subject to conditions. These were in clauses (xiii), (xiiib) and (xiv) of Section 47 of the 1961 Act and are carried into the 2025 Act. Broadly:

**Proprietorship → Company**

- all assets and liabilities of the business become those of the company
- the proprietor holds **at least 50% of the voting power** for **five years** after the succession
- the proprietor receives no consideration other than shares in the company

**Firm → Company**

- all assets and liabilities of the firm become those of the company
- all partners become shareholders **in the same proportion** as their capital accounts
- partners receive no consideration other than shares
- partners together hold **at least 50% of the voting power** for five years

**Private company → LLP**

- all assets and liabilities pass to the LLP, and all shareholders become partners in proportion to their shareholding
- shareholders receive no consideration other than a share in profit and capital contribution
- the company's **total sales, turnover or gross receipts did not exceed ₹60 lakh** in any of the three preceding years, and its **total assets did not exceed ₹5 crore** in any of those years
- no amount is paid to the partners out of the accumulated profits for three years

If a condition is breached later, the exemption is withdrawn and the gain is taxed in the year of breach.

## Points that are easy to miss

- **Stamp duty.** Conversion under the LLP Act or Chapter XXI is generally treated as vesting by operation of law, not as a conveyance. A business transfer by a proprietor, however, can attract stamp duty on immovable property. The position varies by state.
- **GST.** Unutilised input tax credit can be transferred to the new entity through **ITC-02**, where the transfer includes a provision for transfer of liabilities. The new entity needs its own GST registration.
- **Licences and registrations.** IEC, FSSAI, Shop Act, trade licence, Udyam and professional registrations must be updated or re-obtained.
- **Bank accounts and loans.** Get the lender's consent in advance. Loan agreements usually restrict a change of constitution.
- **Contracts.** Check the assignment and change-of-control clauses in customer, vendor and lease agreements.
- **Accumulated losses and depreciation** – the rules on carrying them forward differ by type of conversion.

## Documents usually needed

- financial statements and a statement of assets and liabilities as on the conversion date
- the partnership deed or the company's memorandum and articles
- consent of all partners or the required shareholder resolution
- no-objection letters from secured creditors and lenders
- a list of registrations, licences and bank accounts to be transferred
- the proposed LLP agreement or the articles of the new company
- a capital account or shareholding reconciliation showing how ownership carries across

## What we commonly see in practice

The usual difficulty is not the filing itself but what happens in the years after. Partners or shareholders take out money, change profit-sharing ratios or bring in a new investor within the restricted period without realising that this can withdraw the tax exemption. Old balance sheets often carry revalued assets or partner loans that do not fit the condition that all assets and liabilities pass across. Banks take the longest to update records, so payments to and from the old name continue for months. ## Sequencing a conversion

1. Finalise the target structure and confirm that the tax conditions can be met for the required period.
2. Get lender and key-counterparty consents.
3. Prepare the financial statements as on the conversion date.
4. File the conversion forms and obtain the certificate.
5. Transfer registrations, credit and bank accounts, and notify customers and vendors.
6. Diary the end of the restricted period (five years for most routes, three years for the profit-distribution condition on company to LLP) and review the conditions each year until then.
