If you invoice overseas clients for software, consulting, design or back-office work, the bank now treats your Export Declaration Form (EDF) as filed in many cases. A resident individual is covered for any amount, while a company, LLP or firm is covered only where the inward remittance for the service is ₹10 lakh or less. Above that, the regular EDF within 30 days from the end of the invoice month still applies.

The relief comes from Notification No. FEMA 23(R)/(2)/2026-RB dated 2 November 2026, the Second Amendment to the Foreign Exchange Management (Export and Import of Goods and Services) Regulations, 2026. Although issued in November, it applies from **1 October 2026**, the date on which the 2026 Regulations replaced the old 2015 export regulations.

## What is an EDF for services, and when is it due?

The EDF is the declaration of the full export value that FEMA requires from every exporter. For goods, it is filed at the time of shipment. For services (the Regulations expressly include software within "services"), regulation 3(2) requires it **within 30 days from the end of the month in which the invoice is raised**.

It goes to the "specified authority", which depends on what you export and where you operate:

| Type of export | Specified authority (outside SEZ) | Inside an SEZ |
|---|---|---|
| Software | Authorised dealer bank or STPI | Development Commissioner of the SEZ |
| Other services | Authorised dealer bank | Development Commissioner of the SEZ |

So an invoice raised on 18 October 2026 has an EDF due date of 30 November 2026, unless one of the deemed submission rules covers it.

## Who is now treated as having filed the EDF?

The amendment adds two provisos to regulation 3(2) and a definition: a "resident individual" is a person resident in India who is a natural person.

| Exporter | Deemed to have filed the EDF when | Value limit |
|---|---|---|
| Resident individual (freelancer, professional, consultant in own name) | They give the bank the information needed for crediting the payment to their account | None |
| Anyone else (company, LLP, partnership firm, HUF, trust) | Same condition | Inward remittance for the service not more than ₹10 lakh or the equivalent in foreign currency |
| Non-individual receiving more than ₹10 lakh for a service | No deemed filing | Regular EDF within 30 days from the end of the invoice month |

Two points are easy to miss. First, the deeming is not an exemption: it works only when the payment information actually reaches the bank. Second, for non-individuals the ₹10 lakh test applies to the remittance for the service, not to annual turnover. A firm with ₹2 crore of yearly exports can still benefit for each receipt of ₹10 lakh or less, while a single receipt of ₹12 lakh needs a regular EDF.

Where one invoice is paid in several smaller instalments, the Regulations do not say expressly how the limit applies. Until the bank or the RBI clarifies, it is safer to treat the invoice as a whole and not to assume that splitting the receipts brings it under ₹10 lakh.

## What other EDF options remain available?

The three earlier provisos have been renumbered, not removed:

- An exporter with several overseas recipients in a month can file **one EDF** covering all of that month's exports.
- For services other than software, the EDF can be filed **on or before the date of receipt of payment**.
- The bank can **extend the submission period** on a request that explains the delay, once it is satisfied that the reasons are reasonable.

## How long do you have to bring the money in?

The EDF change does not touch the realisation period. Under regulation 5, as amended on 22 September 2026:

| Situation | Realisation and repatriation period |
|---|---|
| Services invoiced in foreign currency | 9 months from the date of invoice |
| Exports invoiced or settled in Indian rupees | 12 months |
| Project exports | As per the payment terms of the contract |

The bank can extend these periods on a reasoned request. Export receivables can also be set off against import payables with the same overseas party or its group entities, within the realisation period. If proceeds remain unrealised for more than one year after the due date, further exports can be made only against full advance payment or an irrevocable letter of credit.

For small values, regulation 6 allows the bank to permit a reduction in export value, including non-realisation, on the exporter's own declaration where the invoice is up to ₹10 lakh.

## What changed for importers of services?

The same notification substitutes regulation 18(1)(d). Banks must now record an import of services in the Import Data Processing and Monitoring System (IDPMS) only where the importer is not a resident individual and **the invoice or contract value exceeds ₹10 lakh**. The entry must be made within five working days of the bank receiving the details. Note the difference: for exporters the test is the remittance value; for importers it is the invoice or contract value.

## What we commonly see in practice

The paperwork problems with service exports rarely come from the EDF itself. More often they come from how the money is received:

- **Missing purpose details.** Foreign payments land in the account without the bank knowing they are export receipts. The bank then writes to the exporter months later asking for invoices, and the 30-day window has long passed.
- **Invoices that do not match the receipt.** Platform fees, bank charges and currency conversion mean the amount credited rarely equals the invoice. Exporters who do not keep a simple invoice-to-receipt reconciliation struggle to answer bank queries and GST refund scrutiny alike.
- **Several bank accounts.** Advances received in one bank and the balance in another create open entries. The Regulations require an advance and its realisation to go through the same authorised dealer, and a change of bank must be notified to both banks.
- **Late attention to old dues.** Unpaid invoices from overseas clients are often ignored until the realisation period has run out. A request for extension or reduction made before the deadline is far easier to support than one made after.

When banks raise queries, they usually ask for the invoice, the service agreement or engagement letter, proof of delivery of the service such as email correspondence, and a reconciliation of amounts received.

## Does this affect your IEC or GST compliance?

No. The amendment is purely about FEMA reporting through your bank. Zero-rating of export of services and the Letter of Undertaking under GST, and the reporting of foreign income in your return, continue as before. If you also export goods or want benefits under the Foreign Trade Policy, the [Import Export Code and its annual update](../services/import-export-code.html) remain relevant; see also our note on [keeping an IEC active](import-export-code-annual-update.html).

## Dates to watch this winter

October 2026 invoices from non-individual exporters with receipts above ₹10 lakh need an EDF by 30 November 2026, and November invoices by 30 December 2026. Before then, confirm with your bank how it captures the "information required in relation to the receipt of payment" for deemed filing, since each authorised dealer sets its own procedure under its internal policy.
