"Internal audit" and "stock audit" are often confused with the statutory audit. They serve different purposes, are commissioned by different people and report to different audiences.

## Internal audit: who must have one

Under Section 138 of the Companies Act, 2013 and Rule 13 of the Companies (Accounts) Rules, 2014, these companies must appoint an internal auditor:

| Company | Thresholds (any one, in the preceding financial year) |
|---|---|
| Listed companies | All |
| Unlisted public companies | Paid-up capital ≥ ₹50 crore; turnover ≥ ₹200 crore; outstanding loans from banks/FIs ≥ ₹100 crore; or outstanding deposits ≥ ₹25 crore |
| Private companies | Turnover ≥ ₹200 crore; or outstanding loans from banks/FIs ≥ ₹100 crore |

The internal auditor may be a Chartered Accountant, a Cost Accountant or another professional decided by the Board. The internal auditor can be an employee, but must be independent of the statutory auditor's role.

## What a useful internal audit covers

A good internal audit goes beyond checking vouchers. Typical areas:

- **Procure-to-pay** – vendor selection, purchase approvals, three-way matching, duplicate payments
- **Order-to-cash** – credit limits, pricing, discounts, collections, credit notes
- **Inventory** – receipts, issues, physical verification, slow-moving and obsolete stock
- **Payroll** – headcount reconciliation, attendance, statutory deductions
- **Statutory compliance** – GST, TDS, PF/ESI and labour laws
- **IT controls** – user access, segregation of duties, audit trail and backups
- **Internal financial controls** – documented risk-control matrices and testing, which also support the statutory auditor's reporting

The output should be a short list of prioritised observations, each with an owner and a target date, not a thick report nobody reads.

## How internal audit links to the statutory audit

Under the Companies (Auditor's Report) Order, 2020 (CARO), the statutory auditor reports on two points relevant here:

- whether the company has an **internal audit system commensurate with the size and nature of its business**, and whether the internal auditors' reports for the period were considered by the statutory auditor
- where **working capital limits above ₹5 crore** in aggregate are sanctioned on the security of current assets, whether the **quarterly returns or statements** filed with banks agree with the books of account, with details of any differences

A company that files rough stock statements with its bank therefore sees the issue twice: once in the stock audit, and again in the statutory audit report.

## Bank stock audit

Banks lending against inventory and receivables, through cash credit or overdraft limits, appoint stock auditors, usually once or twice a year for larger accounts. The auditor checks:

1. **Stock statements** submitted to the bank, against the books and a physical inspection
2. **Valuation** of stock – at the lower of cost and market value, excluding obsolete, slow-moving or unpaid stock
3. **Receivables** – ageing, and exclusion of debts beyond the bank's eligible period (often 90 or 180 days)
4. **Drawing power** – computed after applying the margins stipulated in the sanction letter
5. **Insurance** – adequacy of cover and the bank clause
6. **Sanction conditions** – compliance with covenants such as stock statement submission and periodic financials

## Getting through a stock audit smoothly

- Submit stock statements that match the books, not rough estimates.
- Keep a **stock register** with locations, and an ageing of receivables.
- Separate out goods held on behalf of others, and goods not yet paid for.
- Keep insurance policies current, with the bank's interest noted.
- Address observations from the previous audit before the next one.

## Documents usually asked for in a stock audit

- sanction letter and the latest stock and book-debt statements submitted to the bank
- stock register by location, with the latest physical verification sheets
- purchase and sales registers for recent months, and the GST returns for the same period
- ageing of debtors and creditors, with lists of related-party and disputed balances
- insurance policies and the valuation method used for stock

## What we commonly see in practice

The most frequent observation is a gap between the stock statement and the books, usually because the statement was prepared from estimates before month-end closing. Next come old debtors still counted towards drawing power, and stock shown at selling price instead of cost. Each of these is easier to fix in the monthly statement than to explain to the bank later.
