Every company in India, from a newly incorporated private company with no revenue to a large listed group, must have its annual financial statements audited. Knowing what the auditor needs makes the process faster and avoids qualifications.

## Appointment of the auditor

- **First auditor:** appointed by the Board within **30 days of incorporation**. If the Board does not do so, the members appoint one within 90 days.
- **Subsequent auditors:** appointed by the members at the AGM for a term of **five years**. The company files **ADT-1** within 15 days of the meeting.
- **Rotation:** listed companies and certain classes of public and private companies must rotate individual auditors after one term of five years and audit firms after two terms.
- **Eligibility:** the auditor must be independent, and must not hold securities in, be indebted to, or have business relationships with the company beyond the limits in Section 141. There are also limits on the number of company audits one partner can hold.
- **Removal or resignation** before the end of the term follows a defined procedure, including filing ADT-2 or ADT-3.

## What the auditor reports on

1. **Opinion on the financial statements** – whether they give a true and fair view under the applicable accounting framework.
2. **Section 143(3) matters** – whether proper books were kept, whether the statements agree with the books, directors' disqualification, pending litigation and other matters.
3. **Audit trail** – whether the accounting software has the edit log feature, whether it operated throughout the year, and whether it was preserved.
4. **Internal financial controls** – for companies other than small companies and private companies below specified thresholds.
5. **CARO 2020** – a detailed report on fixed assets, inventory, loans given, deposits, statutory dues, defaults on borrowings, fraud, related parties, cash losses and other matters.

## When CARO 2020 does not apply

CARO does not apply to:

- one person companies and small companies
- private companies that are not holding or subsidiary companies of a public company, with paid-up capital plus reserves not more than ₹1 crore, borrowings not more than ₹1 crore at any time in the year, and revenue not more than ₹10 crore
- banking, insurance and Section 8 companies

The small company definition was widened by G.S.R. 880(E) dated 1 December 2025: a private company with paid-up capital up to ₹10 crore and turnover up to ₹100 crore now qualifies, unless it falls in an excluded class such as a holding or subsidiary company. More companies are therefore outside CARO and the internal financial controls report than before. Check the classification afresh each year on the latest audited figures.

## How the audit year runs

| Step | Time limit |
|---|---|
| First auditor appointed by the Board | Within 30 days of incorporation |
| Annual general meeting | Within six months of the end of the financial year (first AGM within nine months), so usually by 30 September |
| ADT-1 after appointment at the AGM | Within 15 days of the meeting |
| AOC-4 (financial statements) | Within 30 days of the AGM |
| MGT-7 or MGT-7A (annual return) | Within 60 days of the AGM |

Working backwards, the audit needs to be signed and the Board report approved in time to send notice of the AGM. For a 30 September AGM, that usually means finishing fieldwork in August.

## What we commonly see in practice

- **Audit trail switched off or not enabled for every user.** Smaller companies using desktop accounting packages often discover at year end that the edit log was not running, which has to be reported.
- **MSME dues not identified.** Supplier master data rarely records Udyam status, so the disclosure of amounts due to micro and small enterprises becomes guesswork.
- **Unreconciled statutory dues.** GST and TDS ledgers that do not match the portal create CARO comments on undisputed dues.
- **Loans to directors and related companies** given without checking the restrictions in Sections 185 and 186, or without approvals recorded in minutes.
- **Minutes written months later**, which makes it hard to support related party approvals.

Each of these is easier to fix during the year than in the week before signing. More on the scope of the work is on our [statutory audit](../services/statutory-audit.html) page.

## Preparation checklist for management

- **Closed books:** final trial balance with all year-end entries, including depreciation, provisions, accruals and stock valuation.
- **Bank:** reconciliations and balance confirmations for every account, including closed accounts.
- **Receivables and payables:** ageing, balance confirmations for key parties, and MSME classification of suppliers.
- **Inventory:** a physical verification report, valuation working and an analysis of slow-moving stock.
- **Fixed assets:** register with additions and disposals and the supporting invoices.
- **Statutory dues:** GST, TDS, PF and ESI reconciliations, with proof of payment.
- **Related parties:** a list, and the transactions with them, with Board / shareholder approvals.
- **Minutes and registers:** Board and general meeting minutes, and the statutory registers.
- **Software:** confirmation that the audit trail feature was enabled throughout the year.

A well-prepared audit usually finishes on time. Delays push the AGM and the AOC-4 and MGT-7 filings, which attract additional fees. For companies with a 31 March year end, a practical target is a closed trial balance by the end of June, so that the auditor's queries can be answered well before the notice of the AGM goes out.
